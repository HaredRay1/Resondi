Ausculta: A CNN-Assisted Auscultation Instrument for Lung Sound 
Monitoring, Longitudinal Tracking, and Rehabilitation Support-System
 
Ahnycca Krish N. Malimban 
Science, Technology, Engineering, and Mathematics – Senior High School Department 
Puerto Princesa City National Science High School, Palawan 
 
Abstract—Respiratory illnesses remain a significant health concern, creating a need for accessible and 
systematic respiratory -sound monitoring, particularly in settings where assessment relies heavily on 
conventional auscultation. To address this challenge, the stu dy developed Ausculta, an artificial 
intelligence-assisted smart stethoscope and respiratory monitoring platform designed for standardized 
respiratory-sound recording, recording -quality assessment, sound -pattern classification, longitudinal 
monitoring, and professional review. The system integrates an acoustic stethoscope chest piece, microphone 
and preamplifier, ESP32 microcontroller, Bluetooth Low Energy communication, rechargeable power 
source, and a mobile -first application with guided auscultation usin g six standardized lung locations. 
Respiratory recordings undergo noise reduction, filtering, segmentation, and Mel -spectrogram 
representation before convolutional neural network classification into normal, wheeze, crackle, and 
combined wheeze -and-crackle patterns. The ICBHI 2017 Respiratory Sound Database was used as the 
primary reference dataset for model development and evaluation. Results showed that 68% of recordings 
were rated as good in clarity, while the system demonstrated measurable response times  across its major 
operations. Comparison of 15 paired reference and Ausculta classifications showed 40% agreement, with 
McNemar's exact test indicating no statistically significant difference between the classifications (p=1.000). 
End-user acceptance was also evaluated using a 5 -point Likert scale based on user-friendliness, usability, 
durability, functionality, and community impact. Overall, Ausculta demonstrates the feasibility of 
integrating digital auscultation, AI -assisted sound analysis, and longitudinal monitoring into a single 
platform, providing a practical supportive tool for respiratory assessment and professional review, with 
further validation recommended for actual healthcare settings. 
Keywords: longitudinal tracking, Respiratory monitoring, artificial intelligence, smart stethoscope, respiratory 
sounds, convolutional neural network, auscultation, longitudinal monitoring
I. INTRODUCTION 
   Respiratory conditions such as asthma and 
pneumonia remained major public health concerns 
worldwide, requiring not only medical treatment but 
also continuous rehabilitation and monitoring (World 
Health Organization, 2024). This was especially true 
in the Philippines, where pneumonia ranked among the 
leading causes of death in 2024 and asthma continued 
to affect a significant portion of the population 
(Philippine Statistics Authority, 2025; Philippine 
Council for Health Research and Development, 2023). 
These conditions were made worse by limited access 
to consistent respiratory monitoring and inconsistent 
auscultation practices, particularly in provincial areas 
such as Puerto Princesa City and the wider Palawan 
region. 
     While existing research had established the burden 
of respiratory illness in the country, government 
initiatives had sought to address these gaps through 
digital health technology. The Department of Health 
had prioritized the expansion of telemedicine  and 
remote-monitoring services as part of its digital health 
transformation, aiming to decongest hospitals and 
reach far -flung areas where specialist care remained 
limited (Montemayor , 2022). Locally developed 
biomedical devices, such as the RxBox used to connect 
remote communities to essential diagnostic services, 
had likewise demonstrated the potential of homegrown 
health technology to bridge gaps between healthcare 
providers and patients in underserved areas (OpenGov 
Asia, 2023). Such initiatives highlighted the value of 
accessible, technology-driven tools that could support 
both monitoring and rehabilitation where in -person 
specialist care remained inconsistent. 
   While conventional auscultation remained a valuable 
method for identifying respiratory sound 
characteristics, repeated assessments often varied due 
to differences in stethoscope placement, recording 
conditions, background noise, and individual 
interpretation. Emerging technologies that integrated 
digital stethoscopes with artificial intelligence (AI) 
offered potential for more standardized recording and 
analysis, though their effectiveness depended largely

on recording quality. Inadequate recordings reduced 
the accuracy of AI -supported analysis, emphasizing 
the importance of structured acquisition methods and 
systematic evaluation of recording quality. 
Driven by this gap, this study introduced Ausculta: An 
Intelligent Respiratory Auscultation, Monitoring, and 
Rehabilitation-Support System, an AI -assisted smart-
stethoscope platform designed for standardized 
respiratory-sound acquisition, recording -quality 
assessment, sound -pattern classification, longitu dinal 
comparison, and practitioner review. The system 
incorporated guided auscultation using anatomical 
lung maps, enabling patients to practice independently 
while receiving AI feedback on recording quality, with 
poor recordings repeatable before analysis and current 
recordings comparable to previous ones for 
longitudinal monitoring. AI -generated results were 
intended to serve as decision support rather than 
replace medical-practitioner judgment. 
Despite these existing digital health efforts, the state of 
respiratory rehabilitation in Puerto Princesa City 
revealed ongoing challenges. Recent data from the 
Department of Health -MIMAROPA reported a 19% 
increase in influenza -like illness cases in Palawa n 
during the first half of 2026, with Puerto Princesa City 
accounting for the largest number of infections, 
including hospital admissions and several deaths 
mostly among older adults and individuals with 
underlying conditions (Philippine Information 
Agency, 2026). Environmental factors, including 
occasional haze episodes, further compounded these 
challenges, underscoring the need for a localized, 
technology-driven system to support respiratory 
monitoring and rehabilitation in the area. 
Aligned with the United Nations Sustainable 
Development Goals (UNSDGs), this study supported 
SDG 3 – Good Health and Well -Being by promoting 
accessible respiratory care and rehabilitation; SDG 4 – 
Quality Education by enabling guided, patient -
centered auscultation practice; and SDG 9 – Industry, 
Innovation and Infrastructure by developing a 
technology-driven healthcare device that strengthened 
digital health infrastructure. 
Therefore, this study aimed to: (i) develop and evaluate 
an ESP32 -based smart stethoscope and mobile -first 
platform with BLE and MicroSD communication; (ii) 
guide standardized auscultation using anatomical lung 
maps and capture respiratory sound recordings  with 
metadata; (iii) assess recording quality and preprocess 
sounds for feature extraction; (iv) design and evaluate 
a respiratory -sound classification model using the 
ICBHI 2017 Respiratory Sound Database (Rocha et 
al., 2019); (v) compare device recordin gs with 
reference recordings under clean and noisy conditions; 
(vi) support longitudinal comparison of respiratory 
sound patterns through practitioner review; and (vii) 
incorporate rehabilitation -support functions such as 
controlled, pursed -lip, and diaphragmatic breathing 
under professional guidance. 
Overall, the study sought to develop and evaluate a 
functional prototype that integrated respiratory -sound 
acquisition, quality control, AI -assisted analysis, 
longitudinal monitoring, practitioner -supervised 
review, and rehabilitation support  — contributing to 
accessible, technology-driven respiratory care aligned 
with existing digital health initiatives in the country. 
 
II.      LITERATURE REVIEW 
Respiratory rehabilitation helped patients improve 
their breathing, manage their symptoms, and get back 
their strength and daily function. Cleveland Clinic 
(2024) explained that respiratory rehabilitation was a 
supervised medical program that helped people  with 
chronic lung conditions breathe better, build 
endurance, and improve their quality of life. This 
showed how important accessible tools were for 
supporting monitoring and rehabilitation, especially 
when guided by health professionals. 
According to a study from PubMed Central (2022), 
auscultation was still one of the main ways doctors 
checked lung sounds, but regular stethoscopes did not 
allow sounds to be recorded, saved, or compared over 
time. Digital stethoscopes helped solve this pro blem 
since they allowed lung sounds to be recorded and 
processed for further analysis. Studies also showed that 
artificial intelligence (AI), especially convolutional 
neural networks (CNNs), could classify lung sounds 
like wheezes and crackles fairly accur ately. However, 
things like background noise, poor recording 
technique, and differences between devices could still 
affect how accurate these AI models were, which was 
why proper recording methods and quality checks 
mattered a lot. 
For example, Kim et al. (2021) built a CNN model 
trained on 1,918 lung sound recordings taken from 
actual patients. Their model was able to detect 
abnormal lung sounds with an accuracy of 86.5%, and 
it could further tell the difference between crackles, 
wheezes, and rhonchi  with an accuracy of 85.7%. 
What made this interesting was that the CNN model 
actually performed better than medical students, 
interns, and even some residents who were tested using 
the same recordings. This showed that CNN -based 
<<<PAGE 3>>>

tools could help support doctors instead of replacing 
their judgment, especially in reducing human error 
during auscultation. Similarly, Acharya and Basu  
(2020) created a CNN -RNN model that classified 
respiratory sounds using Mel -spectrograms, and they 
also tested a way to adjust the model for individual 
patients so it could work better for long -term 
monitoring using wearable devices. 
Aside from the AI models themselves, the design of 
the device used to record the sounds also mattered. 
Baraeinejad et al. (2023) developed a digital 
stethoscope that used small microphones (MEMS) and 
Bluetooth Low Energy (BLE) to send real-time audio, 
along with noise -cancellation features to make 
recordings clearer for remote monitoring. Their study 
showed that combi ning the right hardware with a 
mobile app made it easier to capture, store, and view 
auscultation data, and even connect it to electronic 
health records for further AI analysis. 
The ICBHI 2017 Respiratory Sound Database was also 
commonly used in CNN -based studies since it 
provided labeled recordings that researchers could use 
to train and test their models (Rocha et al., 2019). Still, 
there was a difference between how clean these  
reference recordings were compared to ones taken 
from actual devices in real -world settings, which was 
something to consider when testing a new system like 
Ausculta. Digital recordings could also help track 
changes in lung sounds over time, but those chan ges 
still needed to be reviewed by a professional and 
should not be treated as a diagnosis on their own. 
These studies also connected to bigger efforts in the 
Philippines to expand digital health access. The 
Department of Health had been pushing for more 
telemedicine and remote-monitoring services as part of 
its digital health transformation, hoping to ease 
hospital congestion and reach far-off areas that lacked 
specialists (Montemayor , 2022). Locally -made 
devices like the RxBox also proved that homegrown 
health technology could help connect remote 
communities to services they normally could not 
access easily (OpenGov Asia, 2023). These efforts 
showed just how relevant it was to develop accessible, 
CNN-assisted tools for lung monitoring and 
rehabilitation, especially in underserved areas. 
Overall, these findings pointed to the need for one 
system that combined standardized lung sound 
recording, quality checking, CNN-based classification, 
tracking changes over time, and doctor review, along 
with rehabilitation support. Ausculta was built to fill  
this gap by putting all  these functions into one 
supervised platform meant to assist, not replace, 
professional respiratory care and rehabilitation. 
III.       MATERIALS AND METHODS 
 
Materials 
Various materials and instruments supported the 
different phases of the development and validation 
process. The ICBHI 2017 Respiratory Sound Database 
served as the reference dataset for training and testing 
the classification model. Digital modeling softwa re 
and a 3D printer were used to design and fabricate the 
device casing, while an ESP32 microcontroller, 
microphone, preamplifier, rechargeable battery, and 
other electronic components were used to build the 
smart stethoscope prototype. BLE communication 
modules and optional MicroSD storage supported 
wireless data transmission and local recording backup 
during system integration. The Base44 platform was 
used to develop the Ausculta mobile application, 
including its user interface, role-based workflows, and 
research dashboard, while signal -processing tools 
supported noise reduction, filtering, segmentation, and 
Mel-spectrogram conversion for the CNN -based 
classification model. Statistical tools were used to 
compute accuracy, precision, sensitivity, specificit y, 
and F1-score during both manual and device testing. 
Clinical validation involved review by three 
pulmonologists, one of whom was also a pediatrician, 
and device testing among three patients at a private 
hospital under an attending physician's supervisio n. 
Documentation tools, including consent forms and 
data recording sheets, were used to ensure proper 
monitoring and record-keeping throughout the clinical 
validation process. Lastly, a 5 -point Likert scale 
survey was used for end -user acceptance testing 
among 30 participants across Puerto Princesa City and 
the three patients evaluated at the private hospital, 
assessing user -friendliness, usability, durability, 
functionality, and impact on the community.

Reference Sound Collection and Validation  
The ICBHI 2017 Respiratory Sound Database was 
used as the primary public reference dataset for 
training, validation, and testing of the respiratory -
sound classification model. The dataset was treated as 
a respiratory -sound research dataset and not as a 
disease-diagnosis database. Reference labels were 
stored separately from AI predictions. To validate the 
accuracy of the reference lung sounds prior to  model 
training, recordings were reviewed by three 
pulmonologists, one of whom was also a practicing 
pediatrician, allowing classifications to be checked 
against expert clinical judgment across both adult and 
pediatric respiratory sound presentations.

Structural Design Conceptualization  
The researcher consulted an electronics expert from 
Hajimeru ITS for circuit and hardware development 
and prepared a digital model of the stethoscope casing 
and platform architecture. The design prioritized 
reliable sound acquisition, standardized position ing, 
ease of use, and safe separation of acoustic and 
electrical components. 
Components and System Requirements Gathering 
The researcher sourced the microcontroller, 
microphone, preamplifier, communication/storage 
components, battery, and other electronic parts from 
eGizmo and Makerlab Electronics. Materials for the 
casing and assembly were acquired locally where 
practical. 
 
Device Fabrication and Assemblage 
The 3D-printed casing was fabricated and assembled 
at Hajimeru  ITS with the acoustic chest piece, 
diaphragm, microphone, tubing, and electrical 
compartment. The microphone was positioned within 
the acoustic chamber to support respiratory sound 
capture while limiting ambient-noise leakage. 
 
System Integration of Sensing Properties 
The microphone and preamplifier were connected to 
the ESP32, with power and communication 
connections implemented according to the selected 
hardware configuration. Where included, the OLED 
display provided basic device status. The rechargeable 
battery used  an appropriate charging and protection 
circuit. 
 
Smart-Stethoscope BLE Integration 
The ESP32 smart stethoscope communicated with the 
application through BLE. The platform supported 
device scanning, compatible -device identification, 
selection, connection status, audio streaming or 
buffering where supported, recording, and metadata 
storage. 
 
Platform and Database Development 
The Ausculta application was developed using Base44 
to implement the user interface, role-based workflows, 
patient and assessment records, longitudinal 
monitoring interface, medical -practitioner review 
functions, research dashboard, and data -management 
components. The application architecture was 
designed to support integration with the ESP32 -based 
smart stethoscope through a BLE/API integration layer 
and with the dedicated respiratory -sound AI model 
through an AI-analysis interface. 
 
 
Guided Auscultation and Recording Workflow 
Development 
The existing lung map guided recordings from 
standardized anatomical locations: right upper, left 
upper, right middle, left middle, right lower, and left 
lower. The user selected a location, positioned the 
stethoscope, confirmed placement, recorded, and 
saved the audio with its anatomical location and 
recording metadata. Repeated recordings were allowed 
when necessary.  
 
Recording Quality Control Development 
Before AI analysis, each recording was assessed where 
technically possible for background noise, signal 
amplitude, signal -to-noise ratio, duration, clipping, 
motion artifact, missing or invalid samples, and 
insufficient signal. Recordings were classified a s 
Good, Acceptable, Poor, or Invalid. Poor or invalid 
recordings were recommended for repetition rather 
than forced into an AI classification. 
 
Audio Preprocessing and CNN Model Development 
Reference recordings and eligible device recordings 
underwent appropriate preprocessing, including noise 
reduction, filtering, segmentation, and conversion into 
Mel-spectrogram representations where appropriate. A 
dedicated CNN model was developed to class ify 
respiratory sound patterns such as normal, wheeze, 
crackle, and wheeze plus crackle. Additional 
categories such as other/abnormal, uncertain, and 
insufficient quality were used at the platform level. 
The model was not used to independently diagnose 
specific diseases. 
 
Manual (Reference Dataset) Testing 
Reference recordings were processed using the trained 
model to establish baseline classification performance. 
Accuracy, precision, sensitivity, specificity, F1 -score, 
and confusion matrix results were obtained.

Table 3.2  presents the rules applied by the 
interpretation layer. Each rule maps a spatial pattern of 
per-zone classifications to a literature -supported 
possibility, accompanied by its supporting source. 
Patterns matching none of the defined rules default to 
a reco mmendation for further clinical evaluation, 
reflecting the documented finding that lung 
auscultation has high specificity but limited sensitivity 
for acute pulmonary pathologies (Arts et al., 2020). All 
outputs are framed as possibilities requiring clinica l 
evaluation and are never presented as diagnoses. 
Device Unit Testing 
Respiratory sounds captured using the Ausculta 
prototype underwent the same preprocessing and 
classification pipeline. Device recordings were 
compared with reference recordings to evaluate 
consistency and performance under actual acquisition 
conditions, in cluding varying noise conditions. 
Clinical validation of device recordings was conducted 
among three patients at a private hospital in Puerto 
Princesa City, under the guidance and supervision of 
an attending physician who reviewed and consented to 
the procedure. 
 
Longitudinal Monitoring and Practitioner Review 
Testing 
For authorized test cases, current recordings were 
compared with previous recordings from the same 
anatomical location. Changes were reported as 
respiratory sound pattern changes rather than disease 
diagnoses. Medical practitioners were able to review

recordings, recording quality, AI results, symptoms, 
and available manually entered vital signs; confirm, 
disagree with, modify, or request repeat recordings; 
and create monitoring or rehabilitation plans. 
 
Research Dashboard and Data-Export Testing 
The platform provided authorized researchers with de-
identified respiratory sound records, reference labels, 
AI predictions, practitioner confirmations, 
AI/practitioner agreement, and model -performance 
statistics. Research exports exclude unnecessary 
personally identifiable information. 
 
End User Acceptance Testing 
A 5 -point Likert  scale survey assessed user -
friendliness, usability, durability, functionality, and 
impact on community among appropriate prospective 
users, such as health workers and other authorized end 
users. Navigation and feature usability were evaluated 
among 30 par ticipants across Puerto Princesa City. 
Acceptance testing also involved three patients at a 
private hospital, conducted under the guidance and 
supervision of an attending physician who reviewed 
and consented to the procedure. The system's purpose 
and limitations were explained to participants before 
the survey was administered. 
Data Analysis  
 
A. Descriptive Analysis of System and Device 
Specifications 
The specifications of the Ausculta Smart Stethoscope, 
Respiratory Monitoring Platform, and Respiratory -
Sound Analysis System were presented through 
technical specification tables, system architecture 
diagrams, workflow diagrams, screenshots, and 
narrative descriptions. These included the device 
design, components, cost, graphical user interface, 
role-based access control, data management processes, 
audio preprocessing methods, feature representation 
techniques, and classification model architecture. 
 
B. Assessment of Recording Quality and System 
Performance 
Respiratory-sound recording quality was evaluated 
using signal-to-noise ratio (SNR), clarity ratings, and 
noise-level measurements. The response times of the 
smart-stethoscope connection, sound recording, 
recording-quality assessment, signal preprocessing,  
and classification output were likewise measured in 
seconds. Mean and standard deviation were computed 
to summarize the results. 
 
C. Evaluation of Artificial Intelligence Classification 
Performance 
The performance of the respiratory -sound 
classification model was evaluated using accuracy, 
precision, recall (sensitivity), and F1 -score. A paired -
samples t -test was conducted at a 0.05 level of 
significance to determine whether a significant 
difference e xisted between clean and noise -
contaminated respiratory-sound recordings. 
 
D. Comparative Analysis of Classification Outputs 
The classification results generated from reference 
dataset recordings and those obtained from Ausculta 
recordings were compared using accuracy and 
classification agreement measures. A paired -samples 
t-test was employed to determine whether significant 
differences existed between the two sources of 
respiratory-sound recordings at a 0.05 level of 
significance. 
 
E. Comparative Analysis of Recording Quality 
Respiratory-sound recordings acquired using a 
conventional stethoscope and the Ausculta Smart 
Stethoscope were evaluated by qualified healthcare 
professionals using predetermined recording -quality 
criteria. Mean ratings and standard deviations were 
computed, while a paired-samples t-test was applied to 
determine whether significant differences existed 
between the two recording methods at a 0.05 level of 
significance. 
 
F. End-User Acceptance Evaluation 
A 5-point Likert-scale questionnaire was administered 
to assess end -user acceptance of Ausculta in terms of 
user-friendliness, usability, durability, functionality, 
and community impact. Weighted mean and standard 
deviation were computed for each dimension, and the 
results were  interpreted using the established 
acceptance scale.

Figure 4.1. Audio Preprocessing Method of the 
Ausculta Respiratory-Sound Analysis System 
Figure 4.1 illustrates the audio preprocessing method 
used in the Ausculta respiratory -sound analysis 
system. The process begins with the raw respiratory 
audio recording in WAV format, which is first 
subjected to resampling to 16 kHz to provide a 
consistent sampling rate for processing. The audio then 
undergoes noise reduction to minimize background 
interference, followed by amplitude normalization to 
standardize the signal. The normalized recording is 
then divided into fixed-length segments, preparing the 
respiratory sounds for subsequent analysis. The 
resulting preprocessed audio is therefore standardized 
and ready for feature extraction and classificatio n. 
Overall, the preprocessing pipeline organizes the 
recorded respiratory sounds into a consistent format 
suitable for the succeeding stages of the Ausculta 
analysis system. 
 
Figure 4.2. Feature Representation of the Ausculta 
Respiratory-Sound Analysis System 
Figure 4.2  illustrates the feature representation 
process used in the Ausculta respiratory -sound 
analysis system. The process begins with preprocessed 
audio, which is converted into a time -frequency 
representation using the Short -Time Fourier 
Transform (STFT). The resulting representation is then 
transformed into a mel-spectrogram, allowing the 
respiratory sound to be represented as a time -
frequency image. 
The mel -spectrogram is converted into a fixed -size 
feature representation suitable for the classification 
model. This representation is then provided as the 
CNN input, allowing the model to analyze patterns in 
the respiratory sounds. 
Figure 4.3 . Classification Model Architecture of the 
Ausculta Respiratory-Sound Analysis System 
Figure 4.3  illustrates the classification model 
architecture used in the Ausculta respiratory -sound 
analysis system. The process begins with the mel -
spectrogram input, which represents the preprocessed 
respiratory sound as a time-frequency image. The input 

<<<PAGE 12>>>
  
Malimban (2026)                                                                                                                            Page 12 of 83 
is passed through the convolutional layers, which 
extract relevant local patterns from the spectrogram, 
followed by pooling layers that reduce the feature 
dimensions. The extracted features are then processed 
by the fully connected layers, where higher -level 
representations are learned. The softmax layer 
converts the model's output into class probabilities, 
which are then used to determine  the predicted 
respiratory-sound classification. The system classifies 
respiratory sounds into four categories: Normal, 
Wheezing, Crackles, and Wheezing with Crackles. 
Overall, the figure demonstrates the sequence from 
feature input → feature extraction  → dimensionality 
reduction → classification → predicted respiratory -
sound category, showing how the CNN -based model 
processes the audio representation for classification.

V. CONCLUSIONS 
Ausculta demonstrates a structured and integrated 
approach to respiratory-sound monitoring through the 
combination of standardized auscultation, digital 
recording, wireless communication, and ai -assisted 
sound analysis. the system incorporates six 
standardized lung locations and recording -quality 
control to promote more consistent and reliable 
respiratory-sound acquisition. 
 
The integration of the microphone, preamplifier, 
ESP32 microcontroller, and Bluetooth Low Energy 
provides the technical framework for capturing and 
transmitting respiratory sounds. The system also 
incorporates a processing pipeline consisting of noise 
reduction, filtering, segmentation, Mel -spectrogram 
representation, and CNN -based classification of 
normal, wheeze, crackle, and combined wheeze -and-
crackle respiratory-sound patterns. 
 
Overall, the findings demonstrate the feasibility of the 
proposed Ausculta system architecture as a practical 
framework for standardized respiratory -sound 
recording, longitudinal monitoring, and professional 
review. However, the study does not yet provide 
sufficient empirical evidence to establish definitive 
conclusions regarding classification accuracy, 
recording quality, response time, reliability, and user 
acceptance. Therefore, further validation and testing 
are necessary to determine the system's effec tiveness 
and reliability in actual healthcare settings.  
Ausculta is consequently positioned as a supportive 
respiratory-monitoring system that can assist 
professional assessment, rather than as a replacement 
for professional clinical evaluation. Ausculta

of standardized auscultation, digital recording,

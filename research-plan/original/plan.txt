[8|Heading 1] RATIONALE
[9|normal] Respiratory and cardiovascular disease account for the largest share of registered mortality in the Philippines. Of 701,884 deaths registered in 2024, ischaemic heart diseases led all causes at 19.1 percent, and pneumonia ranked fourth at 6.7 percent (Philippine Statistics Authority [PSA], 2026). Auscultation is the first instrumented examination a patient receives in a barangay health station or rural health unit, yet the difficulty is not that lung and heart sounds are inaudible but that they are not retained. A conventional stethoscope produces no record, so nothing survives that a second clinician could review or that could be compared against the same anatomical site at a later visit. In facilities without a resident physician, the assessment of a midwife or barangay health worker is therefore both unverifiable and unrepeatable.
[10|normal] The diagnostic consequence is measurable. In a pooled analysis of thirty-four studies, unaided auscultation achieved a sensitivity of approximately 37 percent against a specificity of 89 percent (Arts et al., 2020), in part because stethoscope placement, listening conditions, and interpretation vary between practitioners (Bohadana et al., 2014). Follow-up care faces a similar gap: pulmonary rehabilitation was available to only 2.7 percent of people with chronic obstructive pulmonary disease worldwide in 2015 (Lahham & Holland, 2021), and change over time is usually tracked through written notes rather than through direct acoustic comparison.
[11|normal] Digital stethoscopes can preserve respiratory and cardiac sounds as recordings (Leng et al., 2015), and convolutional neural networks can help classify them (Rocha et al., 2019; Tariq et al., 2022). However, performance in practice is constrained by background noise and contact artifacts (Shkarupylo et al., 2024), and current evidence does not support autonomous AI interpretation (Park et al., 2025). Commercial electronic stethoscopes also remain costly, with the 3M Littmann CORE listing at roughly ₱22,000 before shipping and duties (3M, 2025), and they do not guide placement or organize recordings by anatomical site for comparison between visits.
[12|normal] This study developed and evaluated Resondi, a low-cost, IOT-based AI-assisted smart stethoscope and mobile cardiopulmonary monitoring platform. Separate Lung and Heart modes guide the user through standardized anatomical sites, store each recording by site on MicroSD, and classify lung sounds with a convolutional neural network. Every AI-assisted result is presented to a licensed practitioner, who may confirm, modify, or return it for a repeat recording. Heart sounds are currently captured and filed for review, with cardiac classification in development. Resondi is intended as a decision-support instrument rather than a diagnostic one, making an examination retrievable and comparable between visits.

[44|Heading 2] Goals
[45|normal] The main objective of the research is to develop and evaluate Resondi, a CNN-fusion-assisted smart auscultation and cardiopulmonary monitoring platform capable of standardized lung- and heart-sound acquisition, recording-quality assessment, cardiopulmonary sound classification, longitudinal monitoring, and medical-practitioner-supervised rehabilitation support. Specifically, the study aims to develop an ESP32-based smart stethoscope and mobile-first platform; establish BLE and MicroSD communication; guide standardized lung and heart auscultation using anatomical locations; capture and store cardiopulmonary sound recordings with metadata; assess recording quality before analysis; preprocess recordings and generate Spectrogram, MFCC, and Chromagram representations; implement a multi-branch CNN feature-fusion model based on the selected reference architecture; compare reference and device-recording performance; provide longitudinal comparison of cardiopulmonary sound patterns; and provide a human-in-the-loop workflow in which medical practitioners can review classifications and approve monitoring or rehabilitation-support recommendations.
[47|Heading 2] Expected Outcomes
[48|normal] The study is expected to produce a functional research prototype that supports standardized cardiopulmonary sound acquisition and CNN-fusion-assisted acoustic analysis under professional oversight. The platform is expected to provide recording-quality indicators, lung- and heart-sound classifications, longitudinal comparison, practitioner review, practitioner-approved monitoring or rehabilitation-support functions, and a de-identified research dashboard for model evaluation. The system will not independently diagnose cardiopulmonary diseases, prescribe rehabilitation, or replace professional assessment. AI outputs will remain decision-support information subject to professional interpretation.

[160|Heading 1] DATA ANALYSIS
[162|Heading 2] Unit of Analysis and Data Partitioning
[163|normal] All analyses in this section will be reported against an explicitly stated unit of analysis. Three units will be used. The respiratory cycle is the unit at which the classification model operates, since the reference database annotates the onset and offset of each cycle together with the presence of crackles and wheezes. The recording is the unit at which the severity score is computed, since a severity percentage is meaningful only in relation to a defined set of breaths from one auscultation location. The patient is the unit at which device-level and longitudinal results are reported.
[164|normal] All partitioning of data into training, validation, and testing subsets will be performed patient-wise, so that no patient contributes data to more than one subset. This is necessary because the reference database contains multiple recordings per patient and multiple cycles per recording; a random split at cycle or recording level would place the same patient on both sides of the partition and inflate every reported metric. Where the official ICBHI 2017 training and testing partition is available, it will be adopted directly so that results remain comparable with published work.
[165|normal] Data augmentation, where applied, will be performed only after partitioning and only on the training subset. Augmented variants of a recording will never appear in the validation or testing subsets.

[170|Heading 2] Respiratory-Sound Severity Algorithm
[171|normal] The researchers will develop a mathematical severity algorithm to quantify the degree of abnormal respiratory-sound activity detected by the Ausculta system on a 0–100% severity scale. The CNN feature-fusion classification model analyses each respiratory cycle and outputs confidence scores between 0 and 1 for four classes: Wheezing, Crackles, Wheezing with Crackles, and Healthy. These confidence scores, rather than the single top label, serve as the input of the severity algorithm, so that faint or uncertain abnormal sounds contribute proportionally less severity than strong, unambiguous ones.

The coefficients will be estimated by ordinary least squares regression, which selects the values minimizing the squared differences between the predicted and reference severity scores:
[184|normal] Because the severity score is a proportion bounded at 0 and 100, and because the four confidence scores sum to one and are therefore structurally collinear, two safeguards will be applied. The regression will be fitted in jamovi with the reference severity score as the dependent variable and the abnormal-class confidence scores as covariates. The dataset will be divided patient-wise into a calibration subset and a held-out validation subset, so that the accuracy of the severity algorithm is evaluated on patients whose recordings were not used to estimate the coefficients. The fitted coefficients will then be fixed and implemented in the Ausculta application.

[215|Heading 2] Kruskal–Wallis Test
[216|normal] To verify that the severity scale meaningfully separates the respiratory-sound categories, the non-parametric Kruskal–Wallis test will be conducted with the predicted severity score as the dependent variable and the reference sound category (Healthy, Crackles, Wheezing, Wheezing with Crackles) as the grouping factor:
[218|normal] where N is the total number of observations, g is the number of groups, n(j) is the size of group j, and R(j) is the sum of ranks in group j. The predicted rather than the reference severity score is used as the dependent variable, because the reference score is computed from the same annotations that define the categories and a test between them could not fail; using the predicted score instead makes the test informative about model performance.
[219|normal] Because a recording may contain cycles of more than one category, each recording will be assigned to a category using an explicit rule stated in advance: a recording is classified as Healthy when no cycle contains an adventitious sound, and otherwise by the most frequent adventitious category among its cycles, with ties resolved in favour of the combined category. The Kruskal–Wallis test is used in place of a parametric one-way ANOVA because the Healthy group exhibits near-zero variance in reference severity, which prevents the computation of variance-based tests. Because the test operates on ranks, it makes no assumptions about group variances or normality. A statistically significant result (p < .05) will indicate that predicted severity differs across reference categories. Pairwise comparisons using the Dwass–Steel–Critchlow–Fligner (DSCF) procedure will identify which specific category pairs differ significantly.

[234|normal] where δ is the equivalence margin. The margin will be specified before data collection and justified in terms of the smallest difference in classification performance that would be clinically meaningful, rather than chosen after the results are known.
[235|Heading 2] Effect Sizes and Correction for Multiple Comparisons
[236|normal] Because statistical significance alone does not indicate the practical magnitude of a difference, every inferential result will be accompanied by an appropriate effect size: eta-squared or partial eta-squared for analysis of variance, epsilon-squared for Kruskal–Wallis, Cohen’s d for paired and independent mean differences, Cramér’s V for categorical associations, and the odds ratio for McNemar comparisons. Ninety-five percent confidence intervals will be reported for all point estimates.
[237|normal] Because performance will be compared across multiple conditions, categories, and auscultation zones, the Holm–Bonferroni procedure will be applied within each family of comparisons, and the number of tests in each family will be stated explicitly so that the adjusted significance threshold is transparent to the reader. All hypothesis tests will be two-tailed and evaluated at α = 0.05.
[238|Heading 2] Rule-Based Interpretation Layer
[239|normal] In addition to the severity score, Resondi applies a rule-based interpretation layer that summarizes the spatial distribution of per-zone classifications. The layer reports acoustic findings rather than disease names: no adventitious sounds detected; wheezing localized to one zone; wheezing distributed across multiple zones; crackles localized to one zone; crackles distributed across multiple zones; and mixed adventitious sounds across multiple zones. Each summary is accompanied by a statement that patterns of this type warrant clinical evaluation, without naming a condition.
[240|normal] This formulation is deliberate. Mapping a spatial pattern directly to a named condition such as asthma or pneumonia would constitute diagnostic inference, which is outside the declared scope of the platform and is not supported by the evidence this study will produce. Where practitioner clinical impressions are available, agreement between the rule-based acoustic summary and the practitioner’s impression will be evaluated using percentage agreement and Cohen’s kappa, with kappa reported as the primary measure.

[244|Heading 2] Heart-Sound Reference Labels
[245|normal] For the cardiac component, ground truth will be taken from the expert annotations of the CirCor DigiScope Phonocardiogram Dataset. Each patient encounter carries a murmur label assigned by a cardiac physiologist — Present, Absent, or Unknown — together with the auscultation locations at which murmur waves were detected and, where present, the murmur’s timing, shape, grading, pitch, and quality. Recordings were acquired at up to four standard locations: the aortic, pulmonary, tricuspid, and mitral valve points. The dataset also supplies manual segmentation boundaries for S1, systole, S2, and diastole.
[246|normal] The Unknown label denotes recordings whose signal quality prevented reliable murmur characterization rather than an intermediate clinical state. These cases will therefore be routed to the platform’s recording-quality pathway rather than treated as a diagnostic category, and the primary cardiac task will be defined as murmur Present versus Absent. The proportion of Unknown cases and the reason for their exclusion will be reported, since discarding them silently would overstate performance under field conditions where poor recordings are common.

[253|Heading 2] Cardiac Classification Metrics
[254|normal] Murmur classification will be evaluated using sensitivity, specificity, precision, F1-score, Cohen’s kappa, and the confusion matrix, reported at both the recording level and the patient level, with the aggregation rule stated explicitly: a murmur is considered present for a patient when it is detected in at least one of that patient’s recordings, consistent with the annotation convention of the source dataset. Accuracy will again be reported alongside the majority-class baseline.
[255|normal] Because the clinical cost of a missed murmur exceeds that of an unnecessary referral, sensitivity will be treated as the primary criterion. The operating threshold will be selected on the validation subset to achieve a pre-specified minimum sensitivity rather than to maximize accuracy, and the resulting specificity will be reported as the cost of that choice. Where comparison with published work on the same dataset is required, the weighted accuracy metric defined for the George B. Moody PhysioNet Challenge 2022 will be reported, with the weighting taken from the Challenge documentation and cited. Where two models or two acquisition conditions are compared by area under the ROC curve on the same cases, DeLong’s test for correlated ROC curves will be used.

[259|Heading 2] Segmentation Analysis
[260|normal] Because murmur interpretation depends on cardiac timing, an S1–systole–S2–diastole segmentation stage will precede feature extraction for the cardiac component, using the expert segmentation boundaries supplied with the reference dataset for training and an automated algorithm at inference time. Segmentation performance will be evaluated separately against the reference boundaries using the mean absolute timing error of detected S1 and S2 onsets, reported in milliseconds.
[261|normal] The effect of segmentation on classification will be quantified by comparing classification performance obtained with expert segmentation against that obtained with automated segmentation. This isolates how much of any classification error originates in the timing stage rather than in the classifier itself.
[262|Heading 2] Hardware Frequency Response Verification
[263|normal] Fundamental heart sounds occupy a substantially lower frequency band than respiratory sounds, with S1 and S2 energy concentrated below approximately 150 Hz, whereas the diagnostically relevant respiratory band extends from roughly 100 to 2,000 Hz. Before any cardiac classification result is reported, the researchers will characterize the frequency response of the complete Resondi acquisition chain — chest piece, tubing, acoustic chamber, MEMS microphone, and preamplifier — by presenting swept sine tones from 20 Hz to 2,000 Hz through a coupled source and measuring the recorded amplitude at each frequency.
[264|normal] The measured low-frequency roll-off will be reported, and cardiac classification performance will be interpreted in light of it. If the acquisition chain attenuates the band containing the fundamental heart sounds, this will be stated as a hardware limitation rather than attributed to the classification model, since no model can recover information the transducer did not capture.
[265|Heading 2] Longitudinal Analysis
[266|normal] For repeated recordings, cardiopulmonary sound classifications, severity and extent scores, recording quality, symptoms, and available manually entered vital signs will be summarized across time. Changes will be described as increasing, decreasing, stable, or inconclusive patterns when supported by the collected data, and a change will be reported as meaningful only when it exceeds the measurement error established by the RMSE of the severity algorithm. These trends will not be interpreted as disease prediction or diagnosis.
[267|Heading 2] Research Agreement Analysis
[268|normal] Where practitioner-confirmed reference labels are available, AI predictions will be compared with the reference labels to determine agreement, disagreement, modification, or uncertainty, quantified using percentage agreement and Cohen’s kappa. Where more than one practitioner reviews the same recordings, inter-rater agreement among practitioners will be computed using Fleiss’ kappa and reported alongside the AI–practitioner agreement, so that AI performance is interpreted against the achievable human baseline rather than against an assumption of perfect human consistency. AI predictions will never overwrite the reference or practitioner-confirmed label.
[269|Heading 2] Software and Reproducibility
[270|normal] Signal processing, feature extraction, and model training will be performed in Python 3 using NumPy, SciPy, librosa, scikit-learn, and TensorFlow. Inferential statistics will be computed in jamovi and cross-checked in Python using statsmodels and pingouin. Random seeds will be fixed and recorded for every partitioning, augmentation, and resampling operation so that the analysis is reproducible, and the analysis scripts together with the fitted coefficients of the severity algorithm will be deposited in the project repository.
[271|Heading 2] Scope Limitation
[272|normal] All analyses described in this section evaluate the detection of acoustic patterns in audio recordings. They do not establish the presence, type, or severity of respiratory or cardiac disease. No output of the analysis pipeline will be presented to patients or used in clinical decision-making. All results constitute decision-support information subject to interpretation by a licensed medical practitioner.
